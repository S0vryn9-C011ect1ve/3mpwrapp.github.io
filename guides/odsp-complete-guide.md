# Complete Guide to ODSP

*Ontario Disability Support Program — who qualifies, how much you get, and what the program does not cover*
*Ontario Disability Support Program — who qualifies, how much you get, and what the
program does not cover*

## Executive Summary

ODSP is Ontario's social assistance program specifically for people with disabilities.
It exists alongside — not instead of — WSIB, CPP Disability, and EI.

**The distinction that catches people:** ODSP is **needs-tested**. It is designed to
top you up to a minimum living standard, not to replace lost income. A person can be
entitled to WSIB *and* ODSP, or CPP Disability *and* ODSP, and still not reach a livable
income — because every one of those programs pays at different rates for different reasons.

**Three things to know before you start:**

1. **ODSP pays less than almost any other program.** If you are eligible for CPP
   Disability, EI Sickness, or WSIB, take those first. They can exist alongside ODSP.
2. **The definition of "disability" is narrower than the word suggests.** It excludes
   anything caused purely by a medical condition that is being treated, in most cases by
   a physician. Mental-health conditions are the most commonly refused category — see
   [Why a claim can be refused](#why-an-odsp-claim-gets-refused).
3. **Applying costs nothing and there is no penalty for applying and being refused.** If
   you are eligible, you should apply. The refusal is what starts the appeal clock, not the
   application.

## How ODSP Fits With Other Programs

Most confusion about ODSP comes from treating it as a fallback when it is actually a
layer. Here is the order that usually matters.

| Program | What it is | Pays | Based on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **WSIB** | Workplace injury insurance | Wage-loss replacement | **Your** past earnings |
| **CPP Disability** | Retirement disability | Flat amount + flat supplement | **Your** contribution history |
| **EI Sickness** | Short-term income replacement | Percentage of insurable earnings | **Your** insurable earnings |
| **ODSP** | Social assistance | **Minimum living standard** | **Need**, not earnings |

**The practical sequence:**

1. **Apply for WSIB if you were injured at work.** ODSP will not cover a workplace
   injury — that is WSIB's jurisdiction, and pursuing the wrong program costs you time.
2. **Apply for CPP Disability** if your condition is long-term and severe enough that you
   cannot work. It is not needs-tested and it does not reduce other income the way ODSP
   does.
3. **Apply for EI Sickness** for short-term illness or injury, including pregnancy
   leave.
4. **Apply for ODSP** to cover the gap — and ODSP can reduce if you receive CPP
   Disability, depending on the amount.

> **A trap worth naming:** applying to the wrong program can delay access to the right one.
> "Why was I refused?" is usually answered by "this was never the right program."
> Use [Which System Applies to Me](/knowledge-base/which-system-applies-to-me/) first if
> you are unsure.

## Who Qualifies

**Basic requirements:**

- You have a disability as defined by the program, and it is expected to last **one year
  or longer**
- You have low income, or your income is not enough to meet basic needs
- You meet the **residential requirements** — generally living in Ontario
- You are **not** eligible for CPP Disability on a long-term basis, or you are and the
  amount does not bring you to the income level ODSP would provide

**Who it is for:** people whose disability is severe enough to limit or prevent work,
and who cannot meet their basic needs through other programs.

**Who it is not for:** people with a short-term condition, people whose condition is
being treated and is expected to resolve, or people whose financial need is not
disability-related.

## The Definition of Disability — and What It Excludes

This is the single most important thing to understand, and the most common source of
refusal.

**ODSP considers you to have a disability if a medical condition or impairment
substantially limits your ability to work or carry out normal daily activities, and it is
expected to continue for at least one year.**

**The exclusion is the part people miss.** An impairment is **not** considered disabling
if it is caused by or related to a condition that is being treated, or is treatable, by
a **physician, by medication, by medical intervention, or by a health professional.**

In practice that means:

- A broken arm expected to heal in six weeks — **excluded**, and correct
- A condition where a doctor is actively managing it and recovery is the goal —
  **commonly excluded**
- A permanent condition, not expected to improve, requiring ongoing management but not
  treatment toward cure — **may be included**

**Mental health is the most difficult category.** A mental-health condition does not
automatically exclude you, but the "being treated by a health professional" test is
applied strictly. Many claims are refused on this ground. If this is your situation, it is
worth getting the question answered in writing *before* you invest months in a
application, and it is a strong candidate for professional representation.

## How Much You Can Receive

ODSP is needs-tested, so the amount depends on your household, not on what you lost.

**Rough guide:**

| Household situation | Generally higher |
|---|---|
| Living alone | Baseline |
| With dependent children | Significantly higher |
| With a partner, no children | Moderate |
| With a partner and children | Highest |

**What people are most often surprised by: the amounts are low.** ODSP is designed to
cover essential living costs, not the cost of disability. It does not pay for a
wheelchair, a vehicle modification, or therapies beyond what is medically required. For
those costs, look to:

- **ODSP Special Implementation** (formerly called Special Assistance) — helps with
  disability-related costs that are not otherwise covered; an application is required
- **Assistive Devices Program (ADP)** — often administered by the **March of Dimes**
- **Trillium Foundation** and other charitable programs

## The Assets Test

Your **assets** can reduce or disqualify your claim. Your income is assessed separately.

Broadly, counts as assets: bank accounts, investments, property you could sell, vehicles
in most cases, and money held by others on your behalf.

**Exempt or protected in many cases:**

- Your primary residence, if you live in it
- One vehicle, in many circumstances
- Registered retirement savings, depending on the program rules
- A lump sum received for a settlement, in some circumstances
- Certain trusts and accounts for a **minor child**

**Deliberate disposal is treated very seriously.** Selling or transferring assets to
qualify is fraud, and the program will seek repayment plus penalties. If your position has
genuinely worsened — assets spent on illness, a vehicle that was written off — say so
with documentation rather than restructuring quietly.

## How to Apply

**Apply through Ontario Social Benefits.** You can apply online, by phone, or in person
at an office.

**What you will need:**

1. **Proof of identity** — government-issued ID, Social Insurance Number
2. **Medical information** — reports from the doctors who have treated you, including
   diagnosis, prognosis, and functional limitations
3. **Financial information** — income, bank balances, assets, housing costs
4. **Housing and household information** — who lives with you, what you pay
5. **Employment information** — whether you worked, and when it stopped

**Two practical notes:**

- **Be complete on the first application.** An incomplete application can be refused
  before it is assessed, and starting again costs you weeks.
- **Keep copies of everything you submit,** with dates. You will need them if you appeal.

## Why an ODSP Claim Gets Refused

The common reasons, roughly in order of frequency:

### 1. The condition is being treated

The single biggest reason. See
[The definition of disability](#the-definition-of-disability--and-what-it-excludes).
If a physician is actively treating you and recovery is the goal, the claim is commonly
refused even though your condition is completely disabling to you.

### 2. The condition is not expected to last a year

The program requires a duration of at least one year. Improvements are assessed as
presented — including your own reporting of a condition that may improve.

### 3. Income or assets exceed the threshold

Unemployment insurance, severance, or a settlement can reduce or end eligibility.
Severance and settlement income is treated specially and often is not what people expect.

### 4. Medical evidence is insufficient

Documentation that does not describe **functional limitations** — what you cannot do — is
routinely treated as insufficient. A diagnosis alone does not establish eligibility. What
matters is what the condition prevents you from doing.

### 5. Not resident in Ontario

If you are living outside Ontario, eligibility changes substantially. You can be eligible
under other provincial or federal programs.

## Appealing a Refusal

**You have the right to appeal, and the request must be made within a set period from the
decision — typically 30 days from the date on the decision letter.** Confirm the exact
period on your letter and treat it as fixed.

**The appeal is not a second application.** It is a request for the decision to be
reconsidered on review. You should respond specifically to the reasons given.

**What strengthens an appeal:**

- **New medical evidence** describing functional limitations the original assessment did
  not address — particularly from a specialist who has actually examined you
- **Clarification of an existing record** rather than an entirely new argument
- **Correcting a factual error** the decision was based on
- **Showing you meet a criterion that was overlooked**, rather than re-arguing one that
  was rejected

**What weakens an appeal:**

- Arguing the program is unfair, or that it is wrong as a system
- Repeating the original application with different words
- New evidence that you could have provided before, without explaining why you did not
- Medical evidence that does not address the stated reason for refusal

> **Get representation for an ODSP appeal.** This is a different process from WSIB, with
> its own rules, and refusals on the "being treated" ground are frequently overturned when
> the right medical evidence is put forward by someone who understands what the program
> needs.

## Working While on ODSP

Working is generally allowed, and it does not automatically end your benefits — but the
rules are specific and getting them wrong is costly.

- There is an **earnings exemption**: you can earn up to a set amount before it reduces
  your benefits
- Beyond that, a portion of your earnings reduces your assistance
- **Employment supports** may be available to help you keep working — accommodation,
  assistive devices, retraining, transportation
- You are expected to tell ODSP about work and income changes

**If a job offer lands while you are on assistance, do not simply start and say nothing.**
Talk to your caseworker first. The rules about exemptions and employment supports can
mean you are better off working with support in place than working and losing benefits.

## If You Are Denied Everything

If you have been refused by WSIB, CPP Disability, EI Sickness, and ODSP, that is
adverse but not the end of it. You have worked through the major programs.

**Remaining routes worth knowing about:**

- **Provincial **: [BC PACE**, **Alberta AISH**, and other provincial equivalents if you
  are a resident of those provinces
- **Federal**: **Canada Disability Benefit** — requires an approved **Disability Tax
  Credit** first, so if you do not have the DTC, that is the first thing to arrange
- **Appeal and judicial review** for any of the above

See [Which System Applies to Me](/knowledge-base/which-system-applies-to-me/) for the
routing, and note that **programs change** — verify current rates and rules against the
official pages below rather than any secondary summary.
